# Evidence-bound resume translation method

This method adapts the useful part of high-density technical-resume writing to medical careers. Its purpose is to uncover and order real evidence, not to make an ordinary contribution sound like ownership.

## 1. Establish a conservative candidate positioning

Write a one-line positioning statement only after enough confirmed evidence exists. It should answer: what domain has this person worked in, what kind of work can they credibly do, and which direction are they pursuing?

Good positioning is specific and modest:

> Clinical research trainee with experience in evidence synthesis and R-based analysis, preparing for hospital research roles.

Do not create a grand title such as “research leader”, “principal investigator”, or “domain expert” from a single course, one short project, or an unconfirmed claim.

## 2. Find the strongest truthful responsibility level

Avoid both extremes: writing every contribution as “participated”, and relabeling a collaborator as an owner.

| Confirmed situation | Appropriate language |
| --- | --- |
| Observed, learned, or attended | observed, received training in, assisted with |
| Completed a defined task under supervision | completed, conducted, maintained, analysed, prepared |
| Chose an approach or owned a bounded module | designed, established, independently completed, took responsibility for |
| Coordinated a workstream or made decisions that shaped delivery | led, coordinated, drove — only with explicit confirmation |

Before using strong language, ask: What did the user decide? What did they design? What problem did they resolve? What output did they personally deliver? If none is confirmed, retain a contribution-level verb.

## 3. Build each experience from evidence, not from a verb list

Use the following order when facts allow it:

```text
Context or research question → personal responsibility → method / tool / technique → verified deliverable or result
```

This is close to STAR, but is more suitable for research and technical work because it keeps the method and the person's scope visible.

For example, after confirmation:

> For a review of {{disease_or_question}}, completed literature screening and data extraction under the project protocol; used R to conduct the confirmed Meta-analysis steps and prepared figures for group discussion.

Do not write a disease area, independent ownership, a full Meta-analysis workflow, or a report unless the fact card confirms each element.

## 4. Place keywords inside proof

Keywords help a reader and ATS recognise relevant experience only when they remain attached to a setting, action, and output.

Avoid a detached list such as:

> R · Meta-analysis · PubMed · qPCR

Prefer a grounded expression such as:

> Used PubMed and Embase under the review protocol to screen studies, and used R for the confirmed analysis and figure-preparation tasks.

The final skill summary may group terms by category, but it must not repeat the same claim already explained in an experience entry.

## 5. Treat numbers as evidence, not decoration

Use a number only when the user can confirm its meaning and source: patient records reviewed, studies screened, samples processed, follow-up cases maintained, presentations delivered, or an analysis output. Never create an approximate count to make a bullet look stronger.

If the user does not remember a number, retain concrete scope instead: disease area, data type, method, experimental system, named deliverable, or stage of a research workflow.

## 6. Translate the same fact for the chosen direction

Use [role packs](role-packs.md) to change the order and emphasis of confirmed facts. For example, the same R-based Meta-analysis may foreground methodological reasoning for academic applications, protocol and data quality for clinical research, evidence interpretation for MSL, or reproducible data workflow for digital-health roles.

Do not generate four versions unless the user selected four directions. A candidate normally needs one primary version and, at most, one alternative.

## 7. Show the user the delta

For each rewrite, show:

- the original selected text;
- the proposed wording;
- the confirmed facts used;
- any omitted detail or `[待补]` item that would change the wording.

The user accepts, edits, or rejects the proposal before it becomes the final resume text.
